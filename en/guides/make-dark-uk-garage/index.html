GUIDE_08 / DARK GARAGE

How to make dark UK garage in Suno: 2-step pocket, short subs, narrow space

Dark garage runs on restraint: a 2-step pocket with human swing, subs that hit and leave, and a space the width of an alley. This guide assembles that prompt block by block.

What makes garage dark

Dark UK garage is defined by what it leaves out. The drums are 2-step: the kick lands on one and skips the places where house would insist, the snare sits displaced off the grid, and shuffled hats carry the motion around the gaps. The pattern skips instead of stomping. If every beat lands, you are already in the wrong genre.

Below that, the sub works in hits, not lines. Short notes that punch and withdraw, tied to the kick, with the silence between them doing half the work. Harmony is one or two minor chord stabs — filtered, cut short, more rhythm than progression. The space is narrow and urban: an alley at night, concrete close on both sides, short reflections, no hall.

Restraint is the engine, not a limitation. The second kick you do not add, the pad you do not sustain, the topline you do not brighten — each absence does genre work. A dark garage prompt spends most of its words fixing what stays out.

Building the prompt block by block

Assemble the prompt from five blocks — genre, drums, low end, harmony, space — and make one decision per block. The drum block deserves the most words, because the 2-step pocket is the first thing the tool tries to normalize; describing drums covers the swing vocabulary in depth.

Assembled, the blocks read: dark 2-step garage, shuffled skipping hats, snare displaced off the grid, human swing, short sub bass hits, minimal minor chord stabs, narrow concrete space, night, instrumental. Adapt each block separately instead of rewriting the line from scratch.

  • Genre: dark 2-step garage, instrumental names the variant and blocks a vocal topline in the same move.
  • Drums: shuffled 2-step drums, skipping hi-hats, snare displaced off the grid, human swing fixes pocket and feel.
  • Low end: short sub bass hits, tight low end keeps the sub percussive instead of melodic.
  • Harmony: minimal minor chord stabs, filtered keys gives the tool a source and a duration.
  • Space: narrow night-time space, concrete, close and dry sets the room without inviting reverb washes.

The axes worth varying

Thirty beats that stay in genre come from moving one axis per attempt while the skeleton holds. Move two blocks at once and you can no longer tell which change did what.

  • Swing amount: from a subtle shuffle to a heavily laid-back, almost drunk pocket. This axis alone separates attempts more than any sound choice.
  • Snare identity: rimshot, clipped clap, or a dry snare with the transient shaved. Same position, different weight.
  • Sub behavior: a single hit under the kick, or a two-note movement answering it. Short notes either way.
  • Stab source: filtered piano, cold organ, detuned keys. One source per attempt.
  • Vocal presence: fully instrumental, or a single pitched-down vocal fragment buried in the space. Nothing brighter.
  • Space depth: dry alley versus wet underpass — the same narrow footprint with more or less tail.

What leaks in and what to exclude

Garage prompts leak toward house. The 2-step gaps invite a four-on-the-floor kick, the stabs invite bright piano-house voicings, and any hint of energy invites festival production: risers, wide supersaws, a euphoric drop. Bright chopped vocals are the other constant intruder — the tool associates garage with them strongly.

A typical exclusion line for this genre: four on the floor, house kick, bright vocal chops, festival, euphoric, uplifting, big room, risers. Exclusions handle identity, not degree — negative prompts covers why banning an element works and banning a quality does not.

How a garage prompt fails

When a dark garage prompt fails, it fails in one of four recognizable directions.

  • The pocket quantizes. The output straightens into stiff house. Spend more words on swing and displacement, fewer on mood, and keep human swing attached to a named element.
  • The track fills up. Pads, arps and a busy topline appear because the tool reads sparseness as unfinished. Name the absences as positive instructions: no pads, kick, snare and hats only.
  • The sub turns into a bassline. Long sliding notes push the track toward bassline and speed garage. Keep short sub hits in the prompt, and restate it if the low end starts to sing.
  • Brightness creeps in. Stabs sharpen and a vocal chop surfaces after the first section. Keep dark, filtered, minor in the prompt and the vocal exclusions in place.
