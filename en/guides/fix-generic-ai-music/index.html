GUIDE_05 / CHARACTER

Why AI music sounds generic and the prompt habits that fix it

Generic output is rarely a mystery. A vague prompt gets averaged into the statistical middle of its genre; concrete words about sources, space and finish pull it out.

Generic is the average, not an accident

When a prompt says almost nothing, the tool fills the gaps with the most common answer. trap beat, hard 808 describes thousands of tracks, so the result lands on their statistical middle: mid-tempo drums, a stock 808, predictable hats, a bright wide master. Nothing is broken. Everything is average.

That is why regenerating with the same words rarely helps. The prompt defines a region, and every attempt is a draw from that region. If the region is the centre of the genre, the draws will keep sounding like the centre of the genre. To move the output, move the words.

The tool is not choosing to be boring. It is answering the question you asked, and trap beat is a boring question.

Climb the specificity ladder

Specificity is a ladder with four rungs. Each rung narrows the region the tool can draw from.

Most generic results come from prompts stuck on the first rung. The guide on how to write music prompts covers the full structure; the ladder is the short version you can apply in ten seconds.

  • trap beat names a genre and nothing else.
  • dark ritual trap, slow, sparse adds scene and era, meaning which corner of the genre and at what pace.
  • Named sound sources fix the material, as in dry drums, dominant 808, detuned bell loop.
  • Space and finish close the direction, as in small dead room, dark master, no reverb tail.

Say what the sounds are made from

The strongest anti-generic lever is the material. Genre words describe a destination; source words describe what the sound is made from, and the tool treats them literally. drums resampled from vinyl, worn cassette loop, field recording air, gritty sampler character each drag the output away from the clean default.

Noir Tapes is built on this lever: every prompt in the folder names sampled sources and a tape treatment before it names anything else.

  • Tape brings saturation, wow and flutter, dropouts and a softened top end.
  • Vinyl brings crackle, dust and the cut of a truncated loop.
  • Field recordings bring room air, street noise and distant traffic under the beat.
  • Machine character brings lo-fi sampler grit and the tone of an early drum machine.

Specify the room and the dirt

Left unspecified, the finish defaults to clean, bright and wide, because that is what most published music sounds like. If every generation comes back with the same polished master, the prompt never said otherwise.

Space words: small dead room, short room reverb only, mono drums, narrow mix. Imperfection words: tape hiss, soft clipping, degraded, dusty. Written in the prompt they are direction, not damage: the tool renders them as character.

Pick one space and one kind of dirt per prompt. Stacking every texture word produces mud, not identity.

Cut the recurring tells with exclusions

Some generic tells survive a specific prompt: bright pop chords, cinematic strings, a vocal hook nobody asked for, a sudden wide glossy drop. For those, the exclusion field works as a scalpel. A schematic list looks like bright pop chords, cinematic strings, wide clean master: three elements left out, not a second prompt.

Write one short list per problem and paste it into the Exclude field, separate from the style text. The negative prompts guide covers where it goes and when to leave the field empty.

Change one axis per attempt

Iteration only teaches you something when you can tell what caused the change. Change one axis per attempt — sources, space, finish or exclusions, never two at once — keep every version next to the prompt that produced it, and compare them cold. That is the discipline behind our method: the 30 prompts in each folder come out of exactly that loop.

The limit is worth stating plainly. A prompt sets a direction; it does not guarantee a result. Two generations of the same text will differ, and some attempts will miss. What these habits change is the floor: the misses land closer to the sound you asked for, and the good draws stop sounding like everyone else's.
