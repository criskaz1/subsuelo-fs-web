GUIDE_03 / DRUMS

How to describe drums in a music prompt and avoid the default groove

The tool always returns a drum pattern; whether it is yours depends on how you name pocket, source and space. “Hard drums” decides none of that.

Why “hard drums” says nothing

“Hard drums” describes how you want to feel about the result. It does not tell the tool what the drums do. Every genre carries a default groove, and a vague adjective leaves that default in place: medium density, quantized pocket, generic kit.

Replace the adjective with three decisions. Pocket is where the hits sit against the grid. Density is how many events fill a bar. Character is what the hits are made of and how much air surrounds them. sparse dry drums, kick and snare only, tight machine pocket fixes all three. “Hard drums” fixes none.

Drum vocabulary fills one slot of a larger structure; the prompt writing guide covers the rest. This guide stays on that one slot.

Machine, break or kit: name the source

Drum words fall into three families, and the tool reads them as signals about the source. Mixing families by accident is the fastest route to a mushy rhythm section.

Pick one family per prompt. If you want a hybrid, say which element comes from where: programmed kick under a chopped break is a decision; three family names in a row are a coin toss.

  • Drum machine character. programmed drums, tight clicky hats, dry machine kick. Useful words are programmed, sequenced, mechanical, gated, clicky. This is the register of Trap Ritual: dry drums, no room, the 808 carrying the weight.
  • Sampled breaks. chopped break, resampled, dusty vinyl drums. Useful words are chopped, looped, resampled, dusty, crackle. Fossil Jungle is built entirely on this family, with breaks degraded on purpose.
  • Played kits. live drummer, room mic, brushed snare, loose fills. Useful words are played, live, room, ghost notes, brushes.

Swing, quantization and human timing

Feel is its own axis, separate from the source. One end reads straight, quantized, rigid, machine-tight. The other reads swung, shuffled, laid-back, behind the beat, loose. These words name a mechanism. “Groovy” and “human feel” name a goal, and the tool has no procedure for a goal.

Attach the feel to a specific element. loose hats, snare slightly behind the beat gives the tool something to execute. 2-step is the clearest case: the pattern lives in hats and snares pulled off the grid, which is why Garaje Oscuro spends its drum lines on swing and skip instead of impact adjectives.

Density: say what the drums do not play

Sparse and busy are decisions, not defaults. On the sparse side: minimal drums, half-time, kick and snare only, no fills, no hat rolls. On the busy side: rolling hats, dense percussion, constant motion, fast chopped breaks. Both directions are legitimate; leaving the choice open is what returns the middle.

Absence has to be named or the tool will fill it. If the pattern should breathe, write the gap into the prompt as a positive instruction. Removing an entire instrument can also go to the exclusion field, but density belongs in the main prompt: it describes the pattern you want, not an element you reject.

The genre word already chose the drums

Genre words carry drum defaults. “Trap” already implies 808, rolled hats and half-time. “Jungle” already implies fast chopped breaks. “Boom bap” already implies a dusty sampled kit. Restating the default wastes space: trap, 808 drums, hi-hat rolls tells the tool nothing it had not assumed.

Spend your drum words on deviations. trap, no hat rolls, sparse percussion changes the outcome because the default did include rolls. And do not fight the genre head-on: jungle, slow live drummer asks for two records at once, and the blend that comes back is one nobody chose.

Four mistakes that flatten a drum prompt

Weak drum prompts tend to fail in one of four ways. Our folders write the drum line of every prompt against these four checks, one genre per folder.

  • Stacking synonyms. punchy hard-hitting banging drums is one vague decision written three times. Swap two adjectives for a pocket word and a density word.
  • Naming incompatible families. live drummer, drum machine, chopped breaks in a single line forces the tool to average them.
  • Describing feel without a target. “Human feel” on its own drifts; loose hats lands.
  • Leaving the rest of the prompt empty. Pocket only reads against bass and space. A precise drum line inside a vague prompt still comes back generic.
